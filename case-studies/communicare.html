Section 01 · The Design Story

From Crisis to Concept

A field-led design process that started with a single sentence from an outreach worker — and ended with wireframes a Father Joe's staff member tested the same afternoon they were drawn.

The inciting incident

In 2023, San Diego passed a regulation banning encampments on public property. The intent was public health. The result, six months in, was something no one had planned for: outreach workers at Father Joe's Villages began losing contact with the clients they had been supporting for months — sometimes years.

When the sweeps moved a person, they moved away from the bench where their case manager met them, the corner where the mobile clinic parked, the shelter that had a bed reserved. 9,300+ people were displaced. About half lost phone access in the same window. Whole care relationships went dark in a weekend.

That sentence became the brief. Whatever we built had to survive the worst version of itself: a person on a borrowed library computer, on a Friday night, trying to reach someone who could help.

Going into the field

We didn't design from desks. The team toured the Father Joe's Villages campus, sat in on intake mornings, and ran structured conversations with the people on both sides of the problem — staff who keep the system running, and clients who depend on it.

  1. Step 01

    Site Tour

    Walked the Father Joe's Villages campus — San Diego's largest provider — to see the spaces, kiosks, and bottlenecks where care actually happens.

  2. Step 02

    Staff Interviews

    15+ conversations with outreach workers, nurses, and case managers across multiple service points and shifts.

  3. Step 03

    Client Sessions

    Group sessions with currently and recently unsheltered people about the texture of their day — and where communication actually breaks.

  4. Step 04

    Synthesis

    Mapped findings into prioritized design opportunities, ranked by what the field had told us was load-bearing.

What we heard

Three barriers showed up in nearly every conversation. None of them were technical. All of them compounded.

01

Phone loss

Clients reported losing roughly a dozen phones a year — to theft, to weather, to the cost of staying paid up. Continuous contact through a personal device is a fiction.

02

Constant displacement

Sweeps don't just move people; they sever the small geographies of trust — the corner, the bench, the parked clinic — that care was built around.

03

Resource blindness

Services exist that clients never reach, because the information is distributed by flyer and word of mouth in a population that moves every few days.

The design response

With the field work behind us, the question stopped being "what's the problem" and started being "what's the smallest thing we can build that the staff can use this Friday." Three artifacts shaped the answer.

Accessibility as foundation

Accessibility wasn't a checklist at the end. It was the floor everything else stood on — because the design assumption was a stranger's device, in a hard moment, with whatever vision and motor ability the user happened to bring that day.

The designs were validated. The wireframes were approved. Now it was time to build.

Section 02 · Building With AI

Iteration in the Age of AI Tools

Four features, four conversations, and an honest account of where AI helped — and where it didn't.

Building this as a junior designer meant my hands had to be on the actual product, not just the screens. That's where AI became real for me. Not "AI did it for me" — AI helped me move faster, question more, and ship things I couldn't have alone.

Claude was my pair for edge cases and trauma-informed copy. Cursor was my pair for plumbing and debugging. Gemini powered the live chatbot. The judgment about what to ship — what stayed, what got cut, what was safe enough to put in front of a vulnerable person on a borrowed device — was still mine to make.

I've had 12 phones stolen this year.

Feature 01 · Access from Anywhere

A login that doesn't require a phone

Secure access codes — no phone number, no email, no personal contact info needed. Clients can sign in from a library terminal, a borrowed phone, or a shelter kiosk and find their account exactly where they left it.

How AI helped here Claude helped me think through edge cases for users on shared devices with no email address — things like silent timeout for an abandoned session, how to handle a code typed wrong three times, and what a recovery flow looks like for someone who can't be reached by SMS.

Every crisis happens on Friday at 6pm.

Feature 02 · Always Someone to Help

Private chat, always-on AI fallback

Private messaging with case managers during business hours, with an always-available AI support chatbot for the nights, weekends, and Friday-at-6 moments when staff are gone. Sensitive messages can be deleted instantly for safety on shared screens.

How AI helped here The 24/7 support chatbot was built on the Gemini API. I used Claude to iterate on the prompt design for trauma-informed responses — specifically: never minimize, never push someone toward a service before acknowledging the situation, and always surface a human escalation path within two messages.

We can't find clients after the sweeps.

Feature 03 · Stay Connected, Stay Safe

Flexible, opt-in location sharing

Clients can share a current location, a future meeting spot, or a general area — whatever they're comfortable with. The mobile health clinic's live position is on the same map, so people can find care without being found.

How AI helped here Cursor helped me debug the location API integration without breaking the accessibility patterns underneath — keeping the map keyboard-navigable and the share controls usable with a screen reader, even as the underlying geolocation logic got rewritten twice.

I don't know what's even available to me.

Feature 04 · Find Help That Fits

Smart resource filtering

Smart filters surface only the resources a person is actually eligible for, based on location, current availability, and stated requirements. No more wasted trips across town to a service that turned out to be closed or full.

How AI helped here Used Claude to generate realistic synthetic data for stress-testing the filter logic across 40+ resources — ID requirements, hours, eligibility quirks, "men only / women only / families only" combinations — to catch the edge cases before a real person hit them at the wrong time.

The principles every AI-assisted decision had to pass

When AI is in the loop, it's tempting to ship whatever works. These four sentences were the gate.

Privacy First

Every interaction gives the user control over what they share, with immediate deletion built in. For vulnerable populations, privacy isn't a feature — it's the basis for trust.

Device Agnostic

Works on any internet-connected screen — library computer, shelter kiosk, borrowed phone. No personal device assumed, ever.

Crisis Ready

24/7 support pathways, because displacement and emergency don't keep business hours. The AI fallback exists for this and only this.

Human Connection

Technology facilitates the relationship; it never replaces it. Every feature points back to a person who can help.
